What has been achieved

Exploratory finding

Some glucose traces are much harder

For all 16 people with at least three rounds of each trace class, errors were larger on diabetic traces.

The same people averaged 9.7 mg/dL more error on diabetic traces. This is evidence of task difficulty, not proof about a player's diabetes status.

Exploratory lead to confirm

A CGM advantage may exist in one group

Among people without diabetes, CGM users did better on non-diabetic traces. Each person had at least six rounds of that trace class.

11 CGM users vs 58 non-users. The difference is about 7.0 mg/dL. It survives the named multiplicity and quality checks, but needs confirmation in new people.

Why this matters: the app usually gives diabetic and non-diabetic players different datasets. A raw difference between player groups combines ability with the difficulty of the task they received.

Our original questions: answered or still open?

H1 · Do people with diabetes predict better?

Skill question still open

A group difference exists, in the opposite direction: average error is 25.9 vs 19.7. But the groups usually see different traces. Within the diabetic trace class, the comparison is smaller and inconclusive.

H2 · Do CGM users predict better overall?

No overall support yet

The overall comparison is inconclusive: 22.2 vs 20.7. The promising non-diabetic subgroup result above is a new lead, not confirmation of the overall hypothesis.

H3 · Does longer diabetes experience help?

Unresolved

No clear relationship yet. Only 25 eligible people have usable diabetes-duration information.

H4 · Does longer CGM experience help?

Benefit not established

The main result does not support a benefit. A rank-based sensitivity check finds a weak, opposite association, which may reflect different traces and player groups.

H5 · Are people better on their own data?

Hardly tested

Only two linked identities completed enough rounds of both kinds. A stricter quality check leaves one. We cannot establish an own-data advantage.

H6 · Do humans beat algorithms?

Pending matched scoring

This audit does not claim a human-versus-model result. We need verified model scores on exactly the windows each participant saw.

A misleading result we caught

Counting browser IDs as separate people produced an apparently strong relationship between CGM experience and own-data accuracy. Linking repeat players shrank that sample from eight IDs to three people; the apparent significance disappeared.

The p-value changed from about 0.009 to 0.431. More games from the same person do not become more independent participants.

How many participants do we need?

The observed spread in scores is about 8–8.4 mg/dL. Plan with a margin of 10 mg/dL, keep the effect we want to detect fixed, and recruit the missing groups deliberately.

320eligible people as an operational target

The groups overlap. One participant fills a diabetes category and a CGM category. A total alone does not fill the gaps.

Own-data recruitment needs its own effort. Keep approximately 70 completed own/generic pairs as an operational goal, with at least six rounds of each kind. The theoretical minimum is 34 only if a 3 mg/dL difference has a paired SD of 6; the current two pairs cannot validate that assumption.

These calculations target 80% power for each primary comparison at two-sided α=0.025. They assume normal mean tests with equally variable groups. Rank tests, shared-window models and interactions may need separate simulation. Do not stop at the first favorable p-value.

What should change next?

  1. Make the comparison fair. Give the groups a balanced, shared set of glucose windows, or explicitly model window difficulty. More participants cannot repair a completely different task.
  2. Improve the dashboard's evidence, not its number of tests. Keep person-level group and paired tests; add uncertainty intervals, explicit eligibility and identity counts, source-aware comparisons, and an exploratory label. Pearson is a valid linear-association test; it cannot answer those other questions.
  3. Keep the original questions, and register new ones separately. The next cohort can test whether CGM benefit differs by diabetes status, how trace difficulty changes performance, and whether accuracy improves across rounds. Decide outcomes, models, exclusions and sample sizes before testing on new people.
  4. Use this snapshot for discovery. Confirm new leads in participants who were not used to discover them. Agree fixed analysis milestones or a valid sequential design; repeatedly checking ordinary p-values changes their meaning.
